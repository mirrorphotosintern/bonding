# Handful — Conversation Games System

**Source:** `Family Conversation Games Report.pdf`  
**Purpose:** Turn the report's strongest oral-game research into a product-ready, zero-prop content system.

> **Global catalog rule:** The catalog is for families worldwide and is not organized around any one country, diaspora, or language. A game with a specific cultural origin may be included only when the app explains it completely, makes it playable for an unfamiliar family, preserves accurate attribution, and offers a globally understandable reason to play.

## 1. What the report changes

The original Handful concept treated conversation as one of six equal activity modes. The report shows that zero-prop oral play is strong enough to become the **fastest entry point and initial product wedge**:

- it works when the family did not plan ahead;
- it needs no inventory, downloads, cleanup, or camera;
- it is naturally suited to cars, restaurants, queues, waiting rooms, and bedtime;
- it is instantly interruptible when food arrives or a journey ends;
- the same game mechanic can be adapted across ages, languages, and cultures;
- success comes from participation, not producing a polished object.

The revised MVP should therefore launch with **Talk Now** as the quickest path from app open to play:

> Open app → choose the situation → hear one line → put the phone down → play.

Hands-on Make, Move, Think, Help, and Perform activities remain important, but conversation games provide the cleanest way to validate whether parents repeatedly return to Handful during unstructured micro-moments.

## 2. Product rules for oral games

Every conversation game must be:

1. **Explainable in one breath.** The adult should be able to begin within 15 seconds.
2. **Zero-prop by default.** Environmental observation is allowed; preparation is not.
3. **Interruptible.** A round can end cleanly at any time.
4. **Turn-balanced.** The adult plays too; the child is not being quizzed.
5. **Expandable.** Include an easier rule, a harder rule, and a child-created remix.
6. **Non-eliminating.** Mistakes create funny turns or rule changes, not exclusion.
7. **Culturally attributable.** Traditional names and origins are preserved without claiming ownership.
8. **Modestly described.** Say what the game asks players to practice; do not promise brain development or emotional regulation.

## 3. Situation-first taxonomy

The report's most useful insight is that games should be selected by the physical and social constraints of the moment, not only by educational category.

### Car or transit

Constraints:

- players are forward-facing;
- movement is restricted;
- scenery may be rich, monotonous, dark, or unavailable;
- the driver must never look at the phone or perform visually demanding actions.

Best mechanics:

- observation;
- yes/no deduction;
- word chains;
- collaborative stories;
- sound and rhythm;
- memory accumulation.

Driver-safe rule: the app is configured before the vehicle moves and becomes audio-only. No game may ask the driver to search for tiny signs, read text, handle objects, or tap controls.

### Restaurant, queue, or waiting room

Constraints:

- low volume;
- small physical footprint;
- uncertain duration;
- immediate interruption;
- nearby strangers and shared objects.

Best mechanics:

- quiet imagination;
- tabletop observation;
- interpersonal questions;
- mnemonic chains;
- finger-scale movement;
- tactile games with consent.

### Bedtime

Constraints:

- low stimulation;
- no competition or escalating volume;
- one adult may be exhausted;
- the game should have a natural ending.

Best mechanics:

- gentle collaborative story;
- gratitude or day reflection;
- slow categories;
- memory;
- “one more turn” limits set in advance.

### At dinner at home

Constraints:

- mixed ages;
- all family members may join;
- conversation should not feel like an interview;
- food and safety take priority.

Best mechanics:

- family knowledge;
- stories;
- absurd dilemmas;
- highs/lows;
- traditions and songs;
- collaborative rather than speed-based play.

### Meltdown recovery or low capacity

Constraints:

- avoid cognitive overload;
- do not present the game as a behavior-management trick;
- connection and co-regulation come before rules;
- the child may decline.

Best mechanics:

- simple noticing;
- two-choice imagination;
- slow imitation;
- quiet sensory prompts with explicit consent.

Handful must never claim that a game will “defuse a tantrum.” The adult can offer it after safety and connection are established.

## 4. Core mechanic families

### 4.1 Observe

Players search a shared environment under one clear constraint.

Examples:

- I Spy;
- Veo Veo;
- Alphabet Hunt;
- Yellow Car;
- Find Something That…

Design strength: the current environment generates fresh content automatically.

Failure mode: visual games can exclude low-vision participants or distract a driver. Every game needs an audio, tactile, or descriptive alternative.

### 4.2 Deduce

One player holds a secret; others narrow possibilities using structured questions.

Examples:

- Twenty Questions;
- Guess the Number;
- Guess Who;
- Secret Rule.

Design strength: high repeatability with no content production.

Age adaptation:

- ages 3–4: two categories and unlimited clues;
- ages 5–6: concrete objects and yes/no questions;
- ages 7–8: broad-to-narrow strategy;
- ages 9–10: limited questions, abstract categories, or logical constraints.

### 4.3 Chain

Each answer must connect to the previous answer by sound, letter, syllable, meaning, song, or verse.

Examples:

- Shiritori;
- Jielong;
- Chengyu Jielong;
- Fazan;
- Kalodont;
- Antakshari;
- Bait Bazi;
- rhyme chains.

Design strength: a universal mechanic that becomes culturally and linguistically distinctive.

Product requirement: the app must not flatten these into an English-only “word chain.” Preserve local rules and offer language-specific variants.

### 4.4 Accumulate

Players repeat an expanding sequence and add one item.

Examples:

- I'm Going on a Picnic;
- Packing a Trunk;
- Ich packe meinen Koffer;
- sound or movement chains.

Design strength: a clear shared state and natural escalating challenge.

Failure mode: elimination punishes younger children and working-memory differences. Handful's default is cooperative: the family helps rebuild the chain.

### 4.5 Transform

Players reinterpret an ordinary object, word, or situation.

Examples:

- This Is Not a Spoon;
- Silly Acronyms;
- alternative endings;
- impossible uses.

Design strength: especially strong in restaurants because the visible environment supplies prompts.

Safety rule: never misuse sharp, breakable, hot, or food-service objects. Imagination can replace physical pantomime.

### 4.6 Alternate

Players build something together while alternating a required frame.

Examples:

- Fortunately / Unfortunately;
- Pass-a-Story;
- word-by-word story;
- question-only conversation.

Design strength: active listening is built into the rule because each turn must incorporate the previous one.

### 4.7 Inhibit

Players must suppress an automatic response.

Examples:

- Chidiya Udd;
- Buzz and Fuzz;
- avoid a forbidden word;
- reverse-answer games.

Design strength: fast, funny, no-prop play.

Product adaptation: replace elimination and penalties with a shared “silly reset,” role swap, or cooperative target.

### 4.8 Reveal

Players share preferences, memories, or experiences through a game frame.

Examples:

- Two Truths and a Lie;
- Would You Rather?;
- Pits and Cherries;
- family Guess Who;
- “when was the last time…?”

Design strength: conversation occurs indirectly, reducing the feeling of an adult interview.

Safety rule: allow “pass,” avoid forced disclosure, and never label a child's answer alarming or wrong in-app.

### 4.9 Rhythm and recitation

Players use song, poetry, clapping, or call-and-response.

Examples:

- Antakshari;
- Bait Bazi;
- Aao Milo Shilo Shalo;
- Veo Veo chant;
- tongue twisters;
- family song chains.

Design strength: carries language, memory, and culture across generations.

Copyright rule: the product can explain the game mechanic but may not distribute unlicensed modern song lyrics or recordings.

### 4.10 Quiet body play

Conversation-adjacent play uses minimal movement or touch.

Examples:

- Finger Dance Party;
- air drawing;
- mirror fingers;
- back drawing.

Consent rule: touch-based games always ask first and include a no-touch alternative such as palm-up air drawing or drawing on a napkin.

## 6. Game card specification

Conversation games need a lighter content object than material activities.

The schema below is illustrated with Twenty Questions. The authoritative authored content is in `PLAYABLE_GAME_CARDS.md`; implementation should convert each reviewed card into this schema rather than infer rules from the index table.

```yaml
id: talk_twenty_questions
title: Twenty Questions
traditionalNames: []
originNotes:
  summary: Widely played deductive guessing game
  sourceUrls: []

fit:
  ageBands: ["5-6", "7-8", "9-10"]
  situations: [car, restaurant, queue, bedtime]
  durationMinutes: [3, 15]
  volume: quiet
  visualDemand: none
  readingDemand: none
  cognitiveLoad: medium
  interruptibility: immediate
  driverSafe: true

start:
  oneBreathRule: "Think of a person, place, or thing. We get twenty yes-or-no questions to find it."
  firstPrompt: "Is it alive?"
  adultModel: "I'll think of something first and show how broad questions help."

play:
  turnStructure: question_round
  cooperativeDefault: true
  passAllowed: true
  endCondition: guessed_or_questions_used

adapt:
  easier: "Choose only animals and give a free clue after every third question."
  harder: "Use only ten questions or choose an abstract idea."
  mixedAges: "The youngest chooses the secret; older players share the question budget."
  childRemix: "Let the child invent the category and number of questions."
  noVision: "Fully playable without sight."
  lowSpeech: "Offer two written or pictured options, or let the player answer by gesture."

close:
  line: "Which question helped us most?"
```

## 7. Interaction design: Talk Now

### Entry

Today screen gains a persistent secondary action:

**Talk Now — no materials**

Tap opens three large situation choices:

- In the car
- Waiting somewhere
- Winding down

The app already knows age and participant count. It immediately selects one game.

### Prompt screen

The entire screen contains:

- game name;
- one-breath rule;
- first line to say;
- optional “easier” and “another one” controls;
- audio readout;
- “Phone down” action.

No explanatory paragraph or developmental claim appears before play.

### Audio-only travel mode

Before a journey, the adult can queue three games. Once travel starts:

- the app speaks the rule;
- the family plays without tapping;
- “next game” is activated by a passenger voice command only if platform capabilities and privacy review permit it;
- otherwise the queued games advance on a passenger tap;
- the driver is never asked to interact.

### Completion

Conversation games do not need a timer. The close appears when the adult returns:

- Played it
- Saved it
- Not for us

Optional: “Their rule was better” saves the child-created remix privately.

## 8. Personalization model

Useful preference signals:

- loved guessing vs storytelling vs wordplay;
- prefers cooperative vs competitive;
- language(s) used for play;
- visual, verbal, musical, or movement preference;
- tolerance for silly vs reflective prompts;
- child-created remix saved;
- context in which the game worked.

Do not infer:

- intelligence;
- vocabulary level;
- emotional diagnosis;
- family relationship quality;
- developmental delay.

## 9. Localization and cultural integrity

Conversation games are not merely translated strings. Their rules depend on language structure.

Examples:

- English word chains often use final letters or sounds.
- Japanese Shiritori uses final kana/mora and the terminal `ん` rule.
- Chinese Jielong may use characters or ending sounds.
- Romanian Fazan uses two-letter strategic traps.
- Antakshari uses the ending consonant of a sung line.
- Bait Bazi uses poetry and literary recall.

Requirements:

- publish each cultural version as its own reviewed game;
- credit the traditional name and cultural context;
- involve fluent reviewers;
- never machine-translate word lists and assume the mechanic still works;
- avoid presenting a living tradition as a Handful invention;
- support family-created house rules.

## 10. Report claims that require caution

The PDF is a useful product-research report, not a systematic academic review. Many citations are blogs, Wikipedia, YouTube, commercial pages, and Reddit. Therefore:

- game descriptions can seed editorial research;
- historical-origin claims require stronger verification before marketing use;
- claims about “critical neurological infrastructure,” regulation, executive function, or literacy should not be repeated as product promises;
- the proposed $5–$10 monthly pricing and return-rate thresholds are hypotheses, not evidence;
- competitor ad longevity does not by itself prove positive return on ad spend;
- a game offered during distress should not be represented as a tantrum treatment.

The product value stands without inflated claims: these games are easy to start, enjoyable to repeat, and capable of turning dead time into shared participation.

## 11. Validation plan

Test the conversation wedge before building the full catalog.

### Concierge pilot

- 30 families
- ages 3–10
- two weeks
- deliver one context-matched game through a lightweight prototype
- no push reminders during the first observation week

### Questions

- Did the adult understand the game in under 15 seconds?
- Did play begin?
- Did it survive interruption?
- Did the child request another round?
- Did the family invent a variant?
- Did the adult return in a different context without prompting?

### Success signals

- 70% of opened prompts lead to attempted play;
- 40% of families return organically in a second context within seven days;
- at least 25% save or repeat one game;
- fewer than 10% report unclear rules;
- zero credible safety incidents;
- qualitative evidence that adults remember game names without reopening the app.

These are pilot targets to test, not forecasts.
