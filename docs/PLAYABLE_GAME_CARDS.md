# Handful — Playable Conversation Game Cards

**Status:** implementation-ready rules; cultural-language games still require fluent review before public release  
**Purpose:** provide enough exact content for an engineer to build Talk Now without guessing what any game means.

## How the app uses these cards

Each card contains:

- the exact instruction shown or spoken;
- a first round that demonstrates the rule;
- turn and ending behavior;
- easier and harder variants;
- a cooperative recovery rule;
- source or provenance notes.

The app displays **How to play**, not the research description. Traditional games retain their names and cultural context. Handful does not reproduce copyrighted lyrics, poems, or recordings.

## Observe and deduce

### 1. I Spy

- **Say:** “I spy something that is blue. Take turns guessing; I’ll give another clue after three guesses.”
- **First round:** Choose a large object everyone can safely see, such as a blue door. Say its color, then add “It is taller than you” if needed.
- **Turns:** Everyone may make one guess before someone guesses again. The finder becomes the next spy.
- **End:** Stop after everyone has been the spy or whenever the wait ends.
- **Easier:** Name color and shape. **Harder:** Use texture, purpose, or relative position without naming color.
- **Recovery:** If nobody finds it, reveal it and let the group choose a better clue together.
- **Safety/access:** A driver does not search. Use “I hear” or “I’m thinking of” when sight is unavailable.
- **Source:** Traditional game; rules summarized in the supplied conversation-games report.

### 2. Veo Veo

- **Say:** One player says, “Veo, veo.” Everyone answers, “¿Qué ves?” The player says, “Una cosita…,” then gives its starting sound or color. Others guess.
- **First round:** “Veo, veo.” / “¿Qué ves?” / “Una cosita que empieza con /m/.” Choose something obvious such as *mesa*.
- **Turns:** One guess per person in rotation. The correct guesser leads next.
- **End:** Everyone leads once.
- **Easier:** Give color plus first sound. **Harder:** Give only a first sound or descriptive riddle.
- **Recovery:** Add a second clue after a full round; reveal after three clues.
- **Safety/access:** Use objects inside the vehicle only if the driver is playing.
- **Source:** Traditional Spanish-language call-and-response form documented in `Family Conversation Games Report.pdf`.

### 3. Alphabet Hunt

- **Say:** “Let’s find the alphabet in order. We need something beginning with A, then B, then C. We can help each other.”
- **First round:** A player finds “apple” or an A on a sign; the next player searches for B.
- **Turns:** One shared sequence; any player may answer after five quiet seconds.
- **End:** Reach Z, reach a chosen target letter, or stop when the setting changes.
- **Easier:** Search for any five chosen letters. **Harder:** Objects only, no printed letters; or use a category such as foods.
- **Recovery:** Skip difficult letters without penalty and remember the skipped letters as a bonus hunt.
- **Safety/access:** Passengers search outside; drivers contribute remembered words rather than scanning signs.
- **Source:** Common travel-game format; original Handful cooperative rules.

### 4. Find Something That…

- **Say:** “Find something that fits my clue: something round, something soft, or something that makes a sound.”
- **First round:** “Find something smaller than your hand.” Each person points to or names a different answer.
- **Turns:** Caller chooses a property; all players answer; the caller chooses the next caller, not a winner.
- **End:** Five clues or one clue from every player.
- **Easier:** Colors and shapes. **Harder:** Relational clues such as “useful in two different ways.”
- **Recovery:** Multiple answers are valid if the player can explain the connection.
- **Safety/access:** Players may describe rather than touch; never handle restaurant equipment or strangers’ belongings.
- **Source:** Original Handful adaptation of environmental scavenger games.

### 5. Twenty Questions

- **Say:** “I’m thinking of a person, place, or thing. Together you get twenty yes-or-no questions to guess it.”
- **First round:** Adult chooses “elephant.” Model: “Is it alive?” → yes. “Is it an animal?” → yes.
- **Turns:** Rotate questions. One shared counter; a direct guess also uses a question.
- **End:** The group guesses correctly or uses question twenty; then reveal and switch chooser.
- **Easier:** Animals only and a free clue after questions 3, 6, and 9. **Harder:** Ten questions or abstract categories.
- **Recovery:** After a vague question, the chooser may ask the player to rephrase it; no turn is lost.
- **Safety/access:** Fully audio-playable and driver-safe.
- **Source:** Traditional deductive game; original cooperative adaptation.

### 6. Guess the Number

- **Say:** “I picked a whole number from one to twenty. Guess; I’ll say higher or lower.”
- **First round:** Secret is 13. Guesses: 10 → higher; 15 → lower; 13 → found.
- **Turns:** Players rotate guesses and may discuss strategy.
- **End:** Find the number, then the finder chooses the next range and secret.
- **Easier:** 1–10 with a visible finger number. **Harder:** 1–100 with at most seven guesses.
- **Recovery:** If an impossible clue is given, rewind to the last correct clue and continue.
- **Safety/access:** Fully audio-playable.
- **Source:** Common number-guessing game; original Handful wording.

### 7. Family Guess Who

- **Say:** “Think of someone our family knows. We’ll ask yes-or-no questions, but not questions about their body.”
- **First round:** Ask “Have we seen them this month?”, “Do they live near us?”, or “Have they taught us something?”
- **Turns:** One question per player, then one optional guess.
- **End:** Guess the person or receive three relationship-based clues.
- **Easier:** Choose from three named people. **Harder:** Include family friends or ancestors known through stories.
- **Recovery:** The chooser may say “I don’t know” and replace a question.
- **Privacy:** Do not use this in public if answers expose sensitive family information.
- **Source:** Original Handful family adaptation of Guess Who/Twenty Questions.

### 8. Secret Rule

- **Say:** “Some things can enter my imaginary club and some cannot. Give examples and work out my secret rule.”
- **First round:** Rule: words with double letters. “Apple can enter; pear cannot.” Players propose “coffee” or “banana.”
- **Turns:** Each person proposes one item; leader says yes/no truthfully.
- **End:** Someone states the rule, or the leader reveals it after three helpful paired examples.
- **Easier:** Visible rules—red things, animals, round objects. **Harder:** Word patterns or two-part rules.
- **Recovery:** If the rule has accidental exceptions, accept the discovery and repair the rule together.
- **Source:** Common induction-game format; original Handful examples.

## Collaborative stories and memory

### 9. Fortunately, Unfortunately

- **Say:** “We’ll tell one story. Turns alternate: ‘Fortunately…’ makes things better; ‘Unfortunately…’ makes them worse.”
- **First round:** “A penguin found a bicycle.” “Fortunately, it fit perfectly.” “Unfortunately, the road was made of jelly.”
- **Turns:** Follow the alternating frame and incorporate the previous sentence.
- **End:** After two turns each, everyone contributes one final “Fortunately” ending.
- **Easier:** Adult supplies the sentence stem. **Harder:** Each turn must reuse an object from two turns earlier.
- **Recovery:** If the sequence gets confused, laugh, name the next frame, and continue.
- **Source:** Established improvisational storytelling game; original Handful cooperative ending.

### 10. Pass-a-Story

- **Say:** “I’ll begin a story with one sentence. Add one sentence that continues what just happened.”
- **First round:** “A tiny door appeared under our kitchen table.” Next player: “Behind it was a mouse wearing boots.”
- **Turns:** One sentence each in a circle.
- **End:** Before starting, choose one round, three rounds, or “until we arrive.” The last player closes with “And from then on…”
- **Easier:** Offer two choices for what happens next. **Harder:** Include a required word on every turn.
- **Recovery:** Contradictions become plot twists; nobody is corrected out of the story.
- **Source:** Traditional collaborative-story format.

### 11. Question-Only Story

- **Say:** “Tell the whole scene using only questions. If someone makes a statement, we turn it into a question together.”
- **First round:** “Why is there a dragon in the pantry?” “Didn’t you invite it?” “Would you like to ask it?”
- **Turns:** One question at a time; it must respond to the previous question.
- **End:** Complete one round or reach a question that naturally closes the scene.
- **Easier:** Questions need not form a story. **Harder:** No question may begin with the same word twice in a row.
- **Recovery:** Statements are not penalties; the group reformulates them.
- **Source:** Improv question-game mechanic; original family-safe rules.

### 12. Change the Ending

- **Say:** “Choose a story everyone knows. We’ll change one decision near the end and invent what happens next.”
- **First round:** “What if Little Red Riding Hood recognized the wolf immediately?” Each player adds one consequence.
- **Turns:** Choose the change together, then contribute one sentence each.
- **End:** Give the new version a title.
- **Easier:** Adult offers two alternative decisions. **Harder:** Keep every character’s motivation believable.
- **Recovery:** Players may use a family story, folktale, or public-domain tale; nobody must remember an exact original.
- **Copyright:** The app supplies no copyrighted story text.
- **Source:** Common alternate-ending prompt; original Handful structure.

### 13. I’m Going on a Picnic

- **Say:** “I’m going on a picnic and bringing apples. Repeat everything in order, then add one item.”
- **First round:** “Apples.” → “Apples and bananas.” → “Apples, bananas, and a kite.”
- **Turns:** Repeat the shared list and add one item.
- **End:** Reach a target of 10 items or complete two turns each.
- **Easier:** Everyone helps reconstruct the list. **Harder:** Items follow A–Z or a secret category.
- **Recovery:** Forgetting triggers a group rebuild, never elimination.
- **Source:** Traditional cumulative-memory game.

### 14. Packing a Trunk

- **Say:** “We’re packing for an imaginary trip. Repeat what is packed, add one thing, and tell us why we need it.”
- **First round:** “I packed a yellow umbrella because it rains marshmallows there.”
- **Turns:** Repeat the ordered list, add an item, give a one-line reason.
- **End:** The suitcase reaches eight items; together decide what destination needs this strange luggage.
- **Easier:** Repeat only the last three items. **Harder:** All additions must suit a chosen climate or begin with successive letters.
- **Recovery:** Group hints are encouraged.
- **Source:** Travel-themed cumulative game related to *Ich packe meinen Koffer*, documented in the supplied report.

### 15. Sound Chain

- **Say:** “Copy our sound pattern, then add one short sound.”
- **First round:** clap → clap, click → clap, click, “boop.”
- **Turns:** Repeat the complete sequence and append one sound made by voice, hands, or a safe surface.
- **End:** Reach eight sounds or complete two rounds.
- **Easier:** Each player repeats only the previous sound before adding. **Harder:** Preserve rhythm as well as order.
- **Recovery:** Rebuild slowly together after a miss.
- **Safety/access:** No startling screams; provide visible gestures or taps for hard-of-hearing players.
- **Source:** Original Handful no-prop accumulation game.

### 16. Movement Chain

- **Say:** “Copy the movements in order, then add one movement that works in this space.”
- **First round:** finger wiggle → finger wiggle, shoulder lift → add eyebrow raise.
- **Turns:** Repeat and append.
- **End:** Eight moves or two turns each.
- **Easier:** Seated finger and face movements. **Harder:** Perform the sequence backward.
- **Recovery:** Group reconstruction, no elimination.
- **Safety/access:** Caller names the available movement range first; no jumping in vehicles, restaurants, or queues.
- **Source:** Common movement-memory mechanic; original Handful constrained-space rules.

## Word, song, and poetry chains

### 17. Word Chain

- **Say:** “Say a word beginning with the final sound of the previous word. Cat ends with /t/, so the next word could be tiger.”
- **First round:** cat → tiger → rabbit → table.
- **Turns:** One word each; names and repeats are allowed for young players.
- **End:** Two complete rounds or a shared target of 20 words.
- **Easier:** Use final letter rather than sound and allow help. **Harder:** Choose one category and ban repeats.
- **Recovery:** If no word appears in five seconds, everyone offers options and the current player chooses one.
- **Source:** Generic English adaptation; language-specific traditions below remain separate games.

### 18. Shiritori — しりとり

- **Say:** “Say a Japanese noun beginning with the final kana sound of the previous noun. Avoid words ending in ん.”
- **First round:** さくら (*sakura*, cherry blossom) → らじお (*rajio*, radio) → おに (*oni*, ogre).
- **Turns:** One Japanese noun per turn; no repeats. Kana, not English spelling, determines the link.
- **Traditional end:** A word ending in ん loses because standard Japanese words do not begin with that mora.
- **Handful default:** Instead of elimination, the group says “ん reset,” chooses a new starter, and continues.
- **Easier:** Show or speak three valid options. **Harder:** Use one semantic category.
- **Release requirement:** A fluent Japanese reviewer must verify every in-app example and edge rule.
- **Sources:** [Shiritori overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiritori) and the supplied report.

### 19. Jielong — 接龙

- **Say:** “Say a Chinese word or phrase whose first character matches the final character of the previous one.”
- **First round:** For a simplified child mode, the fluent reviewer supplies a verified chain and audio examples; players may also agree to chain by ending and beginning sound.
- **Turns:** One item per player; no repeats within the round.
- **End:** Reach 12 links or stop when the group needs a hint.
- **Easier:** Match pinyin syllables and provide three options. **Harder:** Exact-character linking.
- **Recovery:** A fluent player may repair tone or character gently; learners are not eliminated.
- **Release requirement:** Do not generate examples by machine translation. Publish only after fluent review determines character, pinyin, tone, and regional house rules.
- **Source:** [SmileTutor description cited by the report](https://smiletutor.sg/6-games-to-play-to-improve-your-chinese/) and `Family Conversation Games Report.pdf`.

### 20. Antakshari — अंताक्षरी

- **Say:** “Sing a recognizable song line. The next person starts a different song with the final pronounced consonant or sound.”
- **First round:** The family chooses any song they already know; after it ends, the adult names the linking sound aloud so everyone understands the next prompt.
- **Turns:** One short excerpt per person or team; no song repeats in the same game.
- **End:** Everyone sings twice or the family agrees on a final chorus.
- **Easier:** Any language, humming allowed, group help allowed. **Harder:** One language or genre and a 20-second turn.
- **Recovery:** If nobody knows a song, choose a fresh starting sound; no team is eliminated.
- **Copyright:** Handful stores rules and starting letters only—not lyrics, recordings, or an unlicensed song database.
- **Source:** [Antakshari rules](https://anthakshari.ai/how-to-play) and the supplied report.

### 21. Bait Bazi — بیت بازی

- **What it is:** An Urdu poetry-chain game, often played by people who already know Urdu couplets or verses. It is closer to “Antakshari with poetry” than to composing a new poem on the spot.
- **Say:** “Recite an Urdu *sher* or verse you know. The next person recites a different verse beginning with the final pronounced letter of yours.”
- **First round:** The app does **not** invent or quote a poem. A participating adult recites a verse from memory, says the final linking letter aloud, and takes the first response so children can see the pattern.
- **Turns:** One previously learned verse per player or team. Agree before play whether a *sher*, stanza, or single line counts. A verse may not repeat in the same session.
- **End:** For family mode, complete one turn each or six linked verses. Traditional competitive events may end when a team cannot respond, but Handful defaults to collaboration.
- **Easier:** “Poetry Circle”—drop the letter chain; each person shares any remembered two-line poem, nursery rhyme, or family verse. This is the correct beginner mode when the family does not already have an Urdu poetry repertoire.
- **Harder:** Restrict play to one poet, theme, or poetic form.
- **Recovery:** Ask the group for a hint, choose a new linking letter, or switch to Poetry Circle. Never fabricate a verse and attribute it to a poet.
- **Audience:** Recommended for fluent Urdu-speaking families and older children who know verses; it should not be offered as a default English game.
- **Copyright:** Use public-domain poetry, family-recited material, or properly licensed excerpts. The product can explain the mechanic without distributing a verse library.
- **Sources:** [Bait Bazi rules and context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bait_bazi), [Rekhta Baitbaazi event](https://cdn.rekhta.org/events/baitbaazi?lang=ur), and the supplied report.

### 22. Rhyme Time

- **Say:** “I’ll say a word. Take turns saying a real or silly word that rhymes with it.”
- **First round:** cat → hat → splat → “moon-cat” does not rhyme, so the group repairs it to “bat.”
- **Turns:** One rhyme each; repeats allowed for ages 3–4.
- **End:** Find five rhymes, then choose a new starter.
- **Easier:** Adult offers two choices. **Harder:** Build a sentence containing each rhyme.
- **Recovery:** Plausible nonsense words count if the player explains that they are invented.
- **Source:** Traditional rhyme-chain mechanic.

## Fast-rule games

### 23. Chidiya Udd — चिड़िया उड़

- **Say:** “Keep one finger down. When I name something that truly flies and say ‘udd,’ lift it. If it cannot fly, keep it down.”
- **First round:** “Chidiya udd” (bird—lift), “patang udd” (kite—lift), “haathi udd” (elephant—stay).
- **Turns:** One caller gives 8–12 prompts, mixing flying and non-flying things; then choose a new caller.
- **Traditional rule:** Players making the wrong movement may be out.
- **Handful default:** Nobody is eliminated. A mismatch earns a funny “grounded!” reset, and everyone returns their finger to the table.
- **Easier:** Slow pace and obvious animals. **Harder:** Include airplanes, balloons, penguins, ostriches, bats, and ambiguous items agreed on in advance.
- **Safety/access:** Finger movement may be replaced by saying “fly/ground” or using two gestures.
- **Source:** [IIT Bombay D’source Indian Games](https://dsource.in/resource/indian-games/indoor-games/chidiya-udd).

### 24. Buzz and Fuzz

- **Say:** “Count upward. Replace multiples of three with ‘buzz’ and multiples of five with ‘fuzz.’ Multiples of both become ‘buzz-fuzz.’”
- **First round:** 1, 2, buzz, 4, fuzz, buzz, 7…
- **Turns:** One count per person in a circle.
- **End:** Reach 30 together.
- **Easier:** Replace only multiples of three. **Harder:** Choose two new divisors or reverse direction after “buzz-fuzz.”
- **Recovery:** On a mismatch, repeat the last five values together and continue; no elimination.
- **Source:** Family adaptation of the common Fizz Buzz counting game.

### 25. Forbidden Word

- **Say:** “For the next three minutes, nobody says the word ‘yes.’ Ask each other questions that tempt us to say it.”
- **First round:** “Do you like mangoes?” Answer with “I do” rather than “yes.”
- **Turns:** Rotate questioner after every question.
- **End:** Timer ends or everyone asks twice.
- **Easier:** Use a frequent object word during a short imaginary conversation. **Harder:** Ban both “yes” and “no.”
- **Recovery:** Saying the word triggers a group sound and immediate continuation, not a penalty.
- **Privacy:** Personal questions may always be passed.
- **Source:** Common forbidden-word parlor game; original Handful non-eliminating rules.

### 26. This Is Not a Spoon

- **Say:** “Pretend this ordinary thing is something completely different. Show or describe the new use; we guess.”
- **First round:** Without handling anything unsafe, mime a spoon as a tiny guitar, telescope, or boat paddle.
- **Turns:** One reinterpretation each; do not repeat an idea.
- **End:** Ten transformations or everyone goes twice.
- **Easier:** Name rather than mime the new object. **Harder:** Connect each transformation into one story.
- **Recovery:** If no safe object is available, use an imaginary spoon or the player’s hand.
- **Safety:** Never handle knives, hot dishes, glassware, or restaurant property without permission.
- **Source:** Established object-transformation improv game; original safety rules.

### 27. Silly Acronyms

- **Say:** “Choose a short word we can see. Invent a funny phrase using its letters in order.”
- **First round:** MENU → “Monkeys Eat Noodles Upstairs.”
- **Turns:** Work together on one acronym, then rotate who chooses the next word.
- **End:** Make three phrases.
- **Easier:** Three-letter words and group suggestions. **Harder:** The phrase must describe the object or tell a tiny story.
- **Recovery:** Grammar may be silly; the letter order is the only firm constraint.
- **Safety/access:** A driver does not read signs; another player supplies the letters.
- **Source:** Common backronym wordplay; original Handful rules.

## Questions and reflection

### 28. Would You Rather?

- **Say:** “Choose between two playful options, then tell us why. Passing is always allowed.”
- **First round:** “Would you rather have a tiny elephant in your pocket or a giant butterfly as a bicycle?”
- **Turns:** Everyone answers the same prompt; the next player invents a prompt.
- **End:** One prompt per player or five prompts.
- **Easier:** Concrete silly choices. **Harder:** Add “What could change your answer?”
- **Recovery:** There is no correct answer and nobody debates a personal preference.
- **Safety:** Avoid fear, bodies, family separation, money, food insecurity, or humiliating dilemmas.
- **Source:** Common conversational game; original child-safe editorial constraints.

### 29. Two Truths and a Silly

- **Say:** “Tell us three things about yourself: two that happened and one playful invention. We guess the invented one.”
- **First round:** Adult models low-stakes statements such as favorite foods or harmless experiences.
- **Turns:** Speaker gives three statements; each listener guesses once; speaker reveals.
- **End:** Everyone who wants to speak gets one turn.
- **Easier:** Use objects or preferences rather than past events. **Harder:** Make all three equally plausible.
- **Recovery:** “Pass” is always valid; nobody must prove a true statement.
- **Source:** Child-friendly Handful adaptation of Two Truths and a Lie.

### 30. Pits, Cherries, and Seeds

- **Say:** “Share a pit—a hard part of today; a cherry—a good part; and a seed—something you hope for. You may pass any part.”
- **First round:** Adult models ordinary, non-performative answers and does not turn the child’s response into advice.
- **Turns:** One person shares up to three items; listeners may ask, “Do you want listening or ideas?”
- **End:** Everyone is offered one turn; close with thanks, not a lesson.
- **Easier:** Choose only cherry or seed. **Harder:** None; this is reflection, not a challenge.
- **Safety:** The app does not analyze answers. Adults follow normal safeguarding obligations outside the game.
- **Source:** Common rose/thorn/bud reflection pattern; “pit/cherry” form described in the supplied report.

### 31. Things I’m Good At

- **Say:** “Name something you are getting good at, however small. Someone else adds a moment when they noticed it.”
- **First round:** “I’m good at finding lost socks.” Another player: “You found mine yesterday.”
- **Turns:** Each willing person names one ability; another offers concrete evidence.
- **End:** One round, then ask which ability the family wants to learn from each other.
- **Easier:** Include things being learned. **Harder:** Demonstrate or teach the skill later.
- **Recovery:** Passing is allowed; avoid comparing abilities between people.
- **Source:** Adapted from the strengths-list idea in [the ParentLinks source](https://x.com/tomerlondon/status/2008392339308552605?s=20).

### 32. Boredom Menu

- **Say:** “Let’s invent things our family can do when bored: one two-minute thing, one quiet thing, and one energetic thing.”
- **First round:** Adult offers one modest example, then invites the child to reject or remix it.
- **Turns:** Each person contributes one idea; the group assigns it to a menu category.
- **End:** Six ideas. The app may save the text only with adult action; otherwise it remains a spoken game.
- **Easier:** Choose between two sample ideas. **Harder:** Add indoor, outdoor, solo, and together categories.
- **Recovery:** Impractical ideas go on a “someday specials” list rather than being dismissed.
- **Source:** [ParentLinks boredom-list source](https://x.com/tomerlondon/status/2008392339308552605?s=20).

## Rhythm and quiet movement

### 33. Aao Milo Shilo Shalo

- **What it is:** A traditional Indian children’s clapping rhyme with regional wording and gesture variations.
- **Say:** “Face a partner. Speak the rhyme slowly while repeating a clap pattern: own hands, partner’s right hand, own hands, partner’s left hand.”
- **First round:** Learn the four-beat hand pattern without words. Then add the version of the rhyme the family already knows.
- **Turns:** Partners complete one rhyme, swap roles or partners, and optionally increase speed.
- **End:** Three clean rounds or one deliberately silly remix.
- **Easier:** Clap only; no words required. **Harder:** Add both-hands clap or a family-created verse.
- **Recovery:** If hands miss, freeze, laugh, and restart at half speed.
- **Consent/access:** Ask before hand contact. Use mirrored air-claps or tabletop taps as no-touch alternatives.
- **Cultural/copyright note:** Wording varies orally. Handful should not declare one lyric canonical; any supplied text needs fluent cultural review.
- **Source:** [IIT Bombay D’source Indian Games collection](https://dsource.in/resource/indian-games) and [demonstration cited in the report](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yn_IqJ-FlFI).

### 34. Tongue-Twister Ladder

- **Say:** “Choose a short tongue twister. Say it slowly together, then climb three speeds without dropping words.”
- **First round:** Use a public-domain or staff-authored phrase appropriate to the selected language.
- **Turns:** Group attempt, individual optional attempt, then child invents a short alliterative phrase.
- **End:** Three speeds or one invented twister.
- **Easier:** Two-word alliteration. **Harder:** Add rhythm or reverse the phrase.
- **Recovery:** Speech differences are not errors; players can tap the rhythm instead.
- **Copyright/localization:** Store only reviewed original/public-domain phrases, with fluent review per language.
- **Source:** Traditional speech-play format.

### 35. Finger Dance Party

- **Say:** “Only our fingers are dancing. Copy my four-count move, then add your own.”
- **First round:** Tap, tap, wiggle, freeze. Everyone copies; next person replaces one move.
- **Turns:** Leader performs four counts; group mirrors; rotate leader.
- **End:** Everyone leads once.
- **Easier:** Copy one movement. **Harder:** Left and right hands perform different patterns.
- **Recovery:** Any movement counts; there is no synchronization score.
- **Safety/access:** Keep hands away from food, hot dishes, and other people’s space.
- **Source:** Quiet-movement idea from the supplied conversation-games report; original Handful sequence.

### 36. Air Drawing

- **Say:** “Draw a simple shape in the air with one finger. Everyone guesses what it is.”
- **First round:** Slowly draw a large circle, then a house or letter.
- **Turns:** One drawer, one guess per person; drawer gives a category clue after a full round.
- **End:** Everyone draws once or the wait ends.
- **Easier:** Shapes and letters. **Harder:** Objects, actions, or two-part scenes.
- **Recovery:** Drawer may restart larger or use the tabletop without touching it.
- **Accessibility:** For low vision, describe an imaginary object one line at a time; for limited movement, eye direction or verbal tracing can replace finger motion.
- **Source:** No-touch Handful adaptation of back drawing described in the supplied report.
